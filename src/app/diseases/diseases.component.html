Gall Thrips

Gall thrips are tiny, sap-sucking insects that affect black pepper plants by feeding on young leaves and buds. Their activity causes abnormal growths called “leaf galls,” which appear as curled, distorted, or blistered leaves. Infested leaves may also show discoloration, turning yellow or brown over time. These pests are most active during warm and humid conditions, which are common in Sri Lanka’s black pepper growing regions. Severe infestations can reduce plant vigor, hinder photosynthesis, and ultimately decrease yield if not managed promptly.

Identification:-

Leaves develop galls - small, reddish-brown to dark brown protrusions. Leaves may curl inward or show whitish or silvery streaks. Thrips themselves are tiny (1–2 mm), slender, and often found inside the galls.

Life Cycle & Stages , Gall thrips undergo three main stages:-

Early Stage: Initial feeding on young leaves; small galls begin to form. Mid Stage: Galls enlarge and leaf curling becomes more visible; thrips population increases. Late Stage: Galls mature and may dry; heavy infestations can lead to premature leaf drop.

Management Tips:-

Monitor young leaves regularly for early signs of galls. Encourage natural predators such as ladybugs and lacewings. Prune heavily infested shoots to reduce thrips population. Use approved insecticides only if infestations are severe.

Powdery Mildew

When it affects plants, the algal leaf spot is more noticeable. The spots, which can be blotchy or circular in appearance, are often slightly elevated from the surface of the plant. The dots may have feathery or wavy borders. From a crusty grey-green to a greenish-brown, they come in different shades. However, the formation of reddish, spore-producing structures gives the spots a velvety, red-brown look throughout the summer when the alga is reproducing. Premature yellowing and leaf loss are possible in large colonies. The alga colony may only exist briefly in some instances. Under that condition, the plant tissue steadily deteriorates and turns brown. A superficial colony will eventually wash off in the rain or perhaps flake off. Colonies need to break through the plant's surface to live over time, usually through a tiny wound.

Often, algal leaf spot is not a major issue. Remove and destroy spotted leaves as well as sick branches when the illness is moderate. Also, pick up any contaminated leaves that have fallen to the ground and destroy them. Enhancing growth circumstances is a crucial part of treatment since stressed plants are more frequently affected by algal leaf spot. Verify that plants are receiving the right quantity of fertilizer, water, and sunshine. If necessary, remove or thin out surrounding vegetation to enhance air circulation around impacted plants. Wherever possible, steer clear of overhead watering.

Sprays containing copper fungicide are advised when algal leaf spot is severe. If damp circumstances continue, sprays may need to be sprayed every two weeks. Nevertheless, treatment may not be possible for the homeowner and may require expert aid if the damaged plant is too tall to administer sprays properly.

Leaf Blight

Scientific name: Phytophthora capsici (common causal agent of leaf blight in black pepper) Leaf blight is a fungal disease that affects black pepper plants, causing dark lesions on leaves, stems, and sometimes young shoots. It is most severe during warm and wet conditions, which favor the growth and spread of the fungus.

Symptoms:-

Small water-soaked spots on leaves that enlarge over time. Lesions turn brown to black, often surrounded by yellow halos. Infected leaves may curl, dry, and eventually fall off, reducing photosynthesis and plant vigor. In severe cases, stems and shoots can also get infected, leading to dieback.

Life Cycle & Stages, Leaf blight progresses in three main stages:-

Early Stage: Small, pale spots appear on young leaves. Mid Stage: Spots enlarge, darken, and may merge; leaf tissue begins to dry. Late Stage: Extensive leaf death, defoliation, and potential infection of stems or shoots.

Management Tips:-

Regularly inspect plants and remove affected leaves to limit spread. Ensure proper spacing and pruning for air circulation to reduce humidity around plants. Avoid overhead watering, especially during rainy periods. Apply recommended fungicides only when necessary and follow dosage instructions carefully.

Pollu Disease

On immature leaves, little, oval, light yellow-green dots initially develop. The dots are frequently encircled by a constricting yellow zone. Concentric rings with dispersed, small black dots become apparent as the spots enlarge and turn brown or gray, and finally the dry tissue collapses, causing defoliation. Any age of leaves might be impacted.

The principal source of infection is decaying plant detritus, where the disease may thrive. Latent mycelium in the leaf tissue may begin to infiltrate surrounding cells to develop brown patches when young twigs of sensitive cultivars are removed and used to root new plants. This might result in the loss of leaves and twigs. Poor air circulation, high temperatures, high humidity, or protracted leaf wetness periods are conducive to the disease.

Quick Wilt

Scientific name: Phytophthora capsici (major pathogen causing quick wilt in black pepper) Quick wilt is a serious and fast-spreading disease that affects black pepper plants, leading to sudden wilting and death of vines. It is primarily caused by the fungus Phytophthora capsici and is more severe during wet and humid conditions, common in Sri Lanka’s black pepper-growing regions.

Symptoms:-

Sudden drooping or wilting of leaves and shoots, even on healthy-looking vines. Dark brown or black lesions may appear on the stem near the soil line. Roots and base of the stem may rot, leading to rapid plant collapse. Infected vines may die within days if not managed promptly.

Life Cycle & Stages, Quick wilt develops through three main stages:-

Early Stage: Leaves show slight wilting; lower stem may have small dark patches. Mid Stage: Wilting spreads rapidly; dark lesions expand on stems; leaves turn yellow and fall. Late Stage: Severe stem and root rot; entire vine collapses and dies.

Management Tips:-

Remove and destroy infected plants immediately to prevent spread. Improve soil drainage to reduce waterlogging, which favors the fungus. Use resistant varieties of black pepper when available. Apply recommended fungicides as a preventive measure, especially during rainy seasons.

Mosaic

Scientific name: Piper yellow mottle virus (PYMoV)Yellow mottle virus is a viral disease that affects black pepper plants, causing yellowing and mottling of leaves. It reduces plant vigor and can lead to significant yield losses if not managed properly. The virus is mainly transmitted by aphids and contaminated tools.

Symptoms:-

Yellow or light green patches on leaves, often in a mottled or mosaic pattern. Leaves may curl, become distorted, or show stunted growth. Infected plants grow slowly, and shoot tips may die back. Severe infections can lead to reduced flowering and fruiting.

Cycle & Stages, The progression of yellow mottle virus can be observed in three stages:-

Early Stage: Small yellow spots or streaks appear on young leaves. Mid Stage: Yellow patches spread; leaf distortion and curling become visible. Late Stage: Extensive yellowing, stunted growth, reduced yield, and possible death of young shoots.

Management Tips:-

Use virus-free planting materials and resistant varieties where available. Control aphid populations using safe insecticides or natural predators. Avoid mechanical transmission by disinfecting tools regularly. Remove and destroy severely infected plants to prevent the spread.
